# Workshop Plan: Data Science for Software Engineers

Three days, 18 one-hour sessions.
Target audience: second-year undergraduates in computer science or software engineering.
They can write Python, use Git, and remember a little high-school statistics.
They use LLM tools regularly.
They have never used Polars or Altair.

Everything is hands-on. Each session includes either a live coding exercise or
a dataset to explore. Lectures run no more than 20 minutes before participants
touch a keyboard.

Every session includes a replication of a specific claim from a published study
that participants reproduce (or attempt to reproduce) with the session's tools
on the session's data. The replication is often simplified (a subset of the
data, one of several reported results) but it is always tied to an actual
paper. Participants should leave every session having produced at least one
number that appears (in some form) in the published literature.

## Day 1: Working with Real Data

Goal: give participants the tools they need to load, tidy, describe, and
visualize data. Every example comes from an actual SE study.

### Lesson 1: Why Should You Care What Researchers Found?

Motivating question: Are some programmers really ten times more productive than
average?

Replication target: Prechelt [Prechelt2000] - reproduce the box plot of
development time in hours across all languages, and compute the ratio of the
90th to 10th percentile working times.

-   Open with Prechelt's study [Prechelt2000, Prechelt2019]: N programmers, same
    problem, languages of their choice.
    -   Show the raw CSV: what's in it, why it looks the way it does.
    -   Show the box plot of hours worked by hand (draw it on the board first).
    -   Point: "10X" depends entirely on how you measure and who you compare.
    -   Prechelt reports a 105X ratio (best vs. worst); the 90th/10th percentile
        ratio is much smaller. Participants compute both.
-   Begel & Zimmermann [Begel2014] asked hundreds of Microsoft engineers what
    questions they most wanted researchers to answer.
    -   Walk through the top ten: code quality, team productivity, tool effectiveness.
    -   Point: researchers study things practitioners actually care about.
    -   Counter-point: Goodhart's Law - as soon as a measure is used to evaluate
        people, it stops being a useful measure.
-   Devanbu, Zimmermann & Bird [Devanbu2016] found that developers hold strong
    beliefs about what works, but those beliefs often don't match the evidence in
    their own projects.
    -   Point: personal experience misleads. Data helps.
-   Noda et al. [Noda2023] argue that developer experience (DevEx) is
    multidimensional: flow, feedback loops, and cognitive load all matter
    independently. Simple metrics like lines of code per day miss most of what
    makes developers productive or miserable.
    -   Implication: before you measure productivity, you have to decide what
        productivity means, and that choice shapes every number you produce.
-   Workshop map: what we will do over three days and why.

Live coding: Load the Prechelt CSV with Polars. Compute the 10th, 50th, and 90th
percentile of `whours`. Compare the 90th/10th ratio to the 105X claim in the
paper. How do the numbers change if you restrict to Java only?

Exercise 1: Look at the Prechelt CSV. The paper claims the Java range is 3.8-63
hours (ratio 17X). Reproduce that claim: filter to Java rows, find the min and
max of `whours`. Does your result match? If not, explain the discrepancy.

Exercise 2: The Noda et al. DevEx framework [Noda2023] identifies three
dimensions of developer experience: feedback loops, cognitive load, and flow
state. Pick any two programming tasks you did in the last week. For each task,
write one sentence rating it on each dimension (high/medium/low). Then write one
sentence explaining which dimension most limited your productivity and how you
would measure that dimension objectively.

Exercise 3: Compute the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentile of `whours` for each
language in the Prechelt data. Present the result as a Polars dataframe with
one row per language. Which language has the smallest interquartile range? Does
a smaller spread mean the programmers were more skilled, more constrained, or
something else? Write two sentences.

Exercise 4: Goodhart's Law says that once a measure becomes a target, it stops
being a good measure. Pick one metric from the Begel & Zimmermann top-ten list
(e.g., "number of bugs fixed per sprint"). Describe in two sentences how a
developer could game that metric without actually becoming more productive.

Exercise 5: Devanbu et al. [Devanbu2016] found that developers' beliefs about
their own codebase often conflict with what the data shows. Write a two-sentence
hypothesis about why this happens, then describe one study design (what data you
would collect, from whom) that could test your hypothesis.

### Lesson 2: Tidy Data and the Polars Basics

Motivating question: What fraction of Python code on Stack Overflow violates PEP
8's line-length limit?

Replication target: Bafatakis et al. [Bafatakis2019] - reproduce their finding
that a substantial fraction of Python code snippets exceed the 79-character PEP
8 limit, using line-length data from a local Python installation as a proxy.

-   What is tidy data [Wickham2014]?
    -   Each column is one variable; each row is one observation; one table per
        set of observations.
    -   Most real data is not tidy. That is your problem now.
-   Load a CSV with Polars (`pl.read_csv`).
-   Inspect: `shape`, `columns`, `dtypes`, `head`, `describe`.
-   Select columns with `select`; filter rows with `filter`.
-   Handle missing values: what `null` means in Polars; `drop_nulls` vs.
    `fill_null`.
-   Write results back to CSV.
-   Data availability is not guaranteed. Acciai et al. [Acciai2023] ran an audit
    experiment in which they sent data-sharing requests to authors of published
    papers. Response rates were significantly lower when the requester had a
    Chinese-sounding name than an Anglo-Saxon-sounding name. The data you need
    may exist but be inaccessible for reasons that have nothing to do with
    technical barriers.

Live coding: Load the Python file line-length dataset (collected from a local
Python installation, same format as `unused/04_hypotest`). Each row is a file
ID, a line length, and a count of how many lines in that file have that
length. Compute the total number of lines and the number that exceed 79
characters. What fraction of all lines are non-compliant?

Exercise 1: Bafatakis et al. found that compliance varies by file purpose
(library code vs. scripts). Does your data show the same pattern if you split by
directory (e.g., `site-packages` vs. `bin`)? Write the Polars query and report
your finding in one sentence. If your result differs from the paper's, suggest
one reason why.

Exercise 2: The dataset has a `null` value for line length in some rows. Write
two Polars queries: one that drops those rows and one that fills them with 0.
Compute the non-compliance fraction both ways and report both numbers. Write one
sentence explaining which treatment is more defensible and why.

Exercise 3: Compute the number of distinct file IDs in the dataset. Then compute
the number of files where every line is 79 characters or fewer. What fraction of
files are fully compliant? Is the file-level compliance rate higher or lower than
the line-level compliance rate computed in the live coding exercise? Write one
sentence explaining which rate is more useful to report.

Exercise 4: The Acciai et al. [Acciai2023] audit found systematic bias in who
gets responses to data-sharing requests. Suppose you are replicating a study and
the authors have not shared their data despite a published promise to do so.
List two alternative data sources you could use instead, and write one sentence
for each explaining what would be different about your replication as a result.

Exercise 5: Take the tidy line-length data and intentionally make it untidy in
two different ways (e.g., spread the line-length counts into one column per
length value; combine file ID and line length into a single string column).
Write the Polars code to read the untidy version back and restore it to the
original tidy form. Confirm the result by checking that `shape` matches.

Motivating question: How much longer are Python functions than JavaScript

    lesson's tools are exactly what you would need to analyze such data at scale.

Exercise 3: Group the combined dataset by language and compute the 10th, 25th,

Exercise 4: Use `pivot` to reshape the combined dataset so that each row is a

Exercise 5: Compute the ratio of mean lines-per-function for Python to mean

### Lesson 5: Descriptive Statistics - What the Numbers Actually Mean

Motivating question: How many versions does the average Python package have, and
does that summary statistic hide more than it reveals?

Replication target: Reproduce reported descriptive statistics for PyPI release
counts (mean ≈ 11, median = 4, max ≈ 10,797) and explain why the mean and median
are so different.

-   Mean vs. median: when they agree and when they don't.
    -   `ccxt` has nearly 11,000 releases. That pulls the mean up.
    -   Median is 4. That tells a different story.
-   Variance and standard deviation: spread vs. individual data points.
    -   Units: standard deviation has the same units as the data; variance does not.
-   Percentiles: the 10th, 25th, 75th, 90th percentile are more informative
    than min/max.
-   Skewness: when the tail goes right (most SE data does).
-   When to use a log scale: size distributions, response times, bug counts,
    anything that can't go below zero and has a long right tail.

Live coding: Load the PyPI release-count dataset from `unused/03_robust`.
Compute mean, median, standard deviation, and the 10th, 25th, 75th, and 90th

histogram at four different bin sizes and discuss which one is most honest.

Exercise 1: The dataset contains packages with zero releases. Should you keep

Then write one sentence explaining which is more appropriate for answering "how
often do active packages get updated?" and why the choice matters.

Exercise 2: Compute the skewness of the release-count distribution using

Exercise 3: Remove the top 1% of packages by release count and recompute the

Exercise 4: The lesson uses PyPI data. Identify one way in which PyPI packages

## Day 2: Testing Claims

Day 2 is about statistical inference: moving from "what does this sample look
like" to "what can we say about the population, and how confident are we?"

### Lesson 7: The Logic of Hypothesis Testing

Motivating question: Are Python files and JavaScript files actually different
sizes, or could the difference just be noise?

Replication target: The hypothesis testing lesson in `unused/04_hypotest` -
reproduce the finding that line-length distributions differ significantly
between Python and JavaScript (the reported t-statistic is -269.67, p ≈ 0.0).

-   The null hypothesis: "there is no effect; any difference we see is random."
-   The p-value: the probability of seeing a difference at least this large if
    the null hypothesis were true.
    -   It is NOT the probability that the null hypothesis is true.
    -   It is NOT the probability that your result is a fluke.
-   Significance threshold: why 0.05 and why that number is mostly arbitrary.
-   Type I error (false positive) and Type II error (false negative).
    -   Dyba et al. [Dyba2006] reviewed 103 controlled experiments in SE journals
        and found most were dramatically underpowered.
    -   If your study can't detect an effect of realistic size, a "non-significant"
        result tells you almost nothing.
-   p-hacking: if you run enough tests, something will come up "significant."
    -   Bonferroni correction: if you run K tests, require p < 0.05/K.
-   Confidence intervals: a range that contains the true value with probability C.
    -   Usually more useful than a bare p-value.
-   Furia et al. [Furia2022] document a long list of pitfalls in applying
    statistical analysis to SE data: choosing the wrong test for the data type,
    ignoring violations of test assumptions, and reporting p-values without
    checking whether the model fits. Knowing a test exists is not the same as
    knowing when to use it.

Live coding: Load the Python and JavaScript line-length datasets from
`unused/04_hypotest`. Run a two-sample t-test on median line lengths per file.
Compare your t-statistic and p-value to the values in the lesson. They should
match (or be close, depending on how you aggregate). If they don't, find out
why.

Exercise 1: The lesson reports that the t-test gives t = -269.67 on the raw
counts. Reproduce that result. Then: the lesson uses all lines, including very
short and very long ones. What happens to the t-statistic if you restrict to
lines between 2 and 200 characters? Is the finding still significant? Should you
apply a Bonferroni correction here? Write a two-sentence justification.

Exercise 2: Run the same t-test 20 times on random subsamples of the data (each
subsample draws 500 rows with replacement from the full dataset). Plot the
distribution of t-statistics across those 20 runs as a histogram. Write one
sentence describing how stable the t-statistic is and what that implies about
reporting a single run.

Exercise 3: Compute a 95% confidence interval for the difference in mean line
lengths between Python and JavaScript files using `scipy.stats.ttest_ind` with
`alternative='two-sided'`. Report the interval. Write one sentence explaining
why the confidence interval conveys more information than the p-value alone.

Exercise 4: A colleague tells you they tested 30 different pairs of languages
and found five significant differences at p < 0.05. Apply the Bonferroni
correction and report the adjusted threshold. How many of those five results
remain significant? Write one sentence about what this means for interpreting
large-scale comparative studies.

Exercise 5: Write a short function (10-15 lines) that takes a dataframe with a
`language` column and a `line_length` column and returns a dataframe with one

language pairs in the dataset. Report the results and note which pairs would

### Lesson 8: Comparing Two Groups

Motivating question: Do programmers work different hours on weekdays versus
weekends?

Replication target: The large numbers lesson in `unused/05_largenum` - reproduce
the reported means (weekday ≈ 6.8 hours, weekend ≈ 3.2 hours) and the t-test
result (t ≈ 12.8, p ≈ 6.9 × 10^-31).

-   Student's t-test: assumes the data in each group is approximately normal.
    -   `scipy.stats.ttest_ind`
    -   When the p-value is essentially zero, the computer rounds it to 0.0 -
        that's a sign to report it in scientific notation.
-   Mann-Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank-sum): does not assume normality.
    -   Better default for SE data, which is rarely normal.
    -   `scipy.stats.mannwhitneyu`
-   How to choose: plot your data first. If it looks approximately bell-shaped,
    either works. If it's skewed, use Mann-Whitney.
-   Checking normality: QQ plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test.
    -   For large samples, almost everything will fail the normality test because
        the test becomes very sensitive.
-   Fucci et al. [Fucci2018] ran a quasi-experiment with 45 undergraduates: 23
    stayed awake all night before a programming task and 22 slept normally. The
    sleep-deprived group produced lower-quality implementations. This is another
    example of a two-group comparison where the groups differ on exactly one
    controlled variable - which makes causal interpretation much more defensible
    than in observational studies.

Live coding: Load the programmer-hours dataset from `unused/05_largenum`.  Split
into weekday/weekend. Compute means for each group and check them against the
reported values. Run both t-test and Mann-Whitney U. Compare the p-values.  Look
at a violin plot of each group and decide which test is more appropriate.

Exercise 1: You reproduce the t-test result (t ≈ 12.8). Now run the Shapiro-Wilk
normality test on each group. Does the weekend group pass? Does the weekday
group? Given your findings, should you have used the t-test or Mann-Whitney?
Write three sentences justifying your choice, then report what changes (if
anything) in your conclusion.

Exercise 2: The Fucci et al. [Fucci2018] sleep-deprivation study compared two
groups of students. Suppose you had the raw implementation-quality scores for
both groups. Describe in two sentences how you would decide which test to apply
before looking at the p-value, and write the Python code you would run to make
that decision.

Exercise 3: Compute the ratio of the Mann-Whitney U statistic to its maximum
possible value (which is n1 * n2). This gives the common-language effect size
(CLES). Report the value. Write one sentence explaining what CLES means in plain
language for the weekday/weekend comparison.

Exercise 4: Plot a QQ plot for each group (weekday, weekend) by sorting the
observed values and plotting them against the expected quantiles of a normal
distribution. Write two sentences describing what the QQ plot reveals about each
group's distribution.

Exercise 5: Split the programmer-hours data into four groups: Monday-Wednesday,
Thursday-Friday, Saturday, Sunday. Run a Kruskal-Wallis test across all four
groups. Report the test statistic and p-value. Write two sentences interpreting
the result and explaining why you would use Kruskal-Wallis rather than three

### Lesson 9: Effect Size and Practical Significance

Motivating question: Does a statistically significant difference mean the
difference matters?

Replication target: Fucci et al. [Fucci2016] - compute Cohen's d and Cliff's
delta for the productivity outcome in the TDD study. The paper reports a small
effect size for most outcomes; participants should verify that claim and report
whether it changes their interpretation of the p-value.

-   Statistical significance is not practical significance.
    -   With N = 100,000, even a difference of 0.001 can be "significant."
    -   With N = 10, even a large difference may not reach significance.
-   Cohen's d: standardized difference between means.
    -   Small ≈ 0.2, medium ≈ 0.5, large ≈ 0.8 (these are rough heuristics).
-   Common-language effect size (CLES): the probability that a randomly chosen
    value from group A is larger than a randomly chosen value from group B.
    -   More interpretable than Cohen's d for non-statisticians.
-   Cliff's delta: non-parametric effect size for ordinal data.
-   DeOliveiraNeto et al. [DeOliveiraNeto2019] reviewed 5,000+ SE papers and
    found that very few report effect sizes - a major gap in the field.
-   Furia et al. [Furia2022] make a related point: standard frequentist analyses
    of SE data often lead to conflicting conclusions depending on how confounds
    are handled. Reporting only a p-value, without an effect size and a
    sensitivity analysis, leaves readers unable to judge whether the finding is
    robust.

Live coding: Load the Fucci TDD dataset. Compute Cohen's d and Cliff's delta for
the `PROD` (productivity) column comparing TDD and TLD groups. Do the effect
size values match the paper's description of a "small" effect? Now do the same
for `QLTY` (quality) and `TESTS` (test count). Which outcome has the largest
practical effect?

Exercise 1: Compute Cohen's d for the weekday/weekend programmer-hours data from
Session 8. The t-test found p ≈ 10^-31. What is d? Is the effect "small,"
"medium," or "large" by Cohen's rough heuristic? Does that change your advice to
a manager who is considering banning weekend work? Write two sentences.

Exercise 2: Compute CLES for the weekday/weekend comparison. Write one sentence
interpreting the value in plain English (e.g., "if you pick one weekday
observation and one weekend observation at random, the weekday value will be
larger X% of the time"). Then write one sentence explaining why this is easier
to explain to a non-statistician than Cohen's d.

Exercise 3: Construct a synthetic dataset where Cohen's d = 0.8 (large) but
p > 0.05. Write the Python code that generates the data and runs the t-test,
and report the sample size you needed to make this happen. Write one sentence
explaining what this demonstrates about the relationship between effect size and
statistical power.

Exercise 4: DeOliveiraNeto et al. [DeOliveiraNeto2019] found that most SE papers
do not report effect sizes. Find one of the p-values reported in the Prechelt
analysis from Session 6 and compute the corresponding Cohen's d. Write two
sentences arguing whether the effect size strengthens or weakens the paper's
main claim.

Exercise 5: Compute Cliff's delta for all three outcome variables in the Fucci
TDD dataset (TESTS, QLTY, PROD). Present the results in a small table with
columns for outcome, Cliff's delta, and a qualitative label (negligible, small,
medium, large using the standard thresholds). Write one sentence summarizing

### Lesson 10: Correlation and Prediction

Motivating question: Can we predict the number of functions in a file just by
counting lines?

Replication target: The correlation study in `unused/correlation` - reproduce
the reported Pearson correlation coefficients (Python r ≈ 0.806, JavaScript r ≈
0.728) between lines per file and functions per file.

-   Pearson correlation coefficient r: always between -1 and 1.
    -   r = 0.8 for Python (lines vs. functions); r = 0.73 for JavaScript.
    -   Does NOT imply causation.
    -   Can be misleading if the relationship is non-linear.
-   Spearman rank correlation: use when data is ordinal or heavily skewed.
-   Always plot before computing: `mark_point` scatter, then add a trend line.

### Lesson 11: Confounds, Bias, and Threats to Validity

Motivating question: Does code ownership improve software quality, and how would
we know?

Replication target: Kalliamvakou et al. [Kalliamvakou2014] - reproduce (on a
small random sample) the finding that a large fraction of GitHub repositories
are not active software projects: personal experiments, mirrors, homework, or
abandoned stubs.

-   Bird et al. [Bird2011] found that files with many contributors have more bugs.
    -   Specifically, in an analysis of Windows Vista and Windows 7, files with
        low ownership (many contributors, none dominant) had significantly higher
        post-release defect rates than files with a clear primary owner.
    -   But: larger files have more bugs too. Is ownership the cause, or file size?
    -   Confound: a third variable that affects both the cause and the effect.
-   Selection bias: GitHub data over-represents successful projects.
    -   Kalliamvakou et al. [Kalliamvakou2014] found that most "projects" are
        personal experiments, mirrors, or abandoned repos - far from representative.
-   Survivorship bias: we study software that still exists.
    -   Ait et al. [Ait2022] analyzed early development dynamics in GitHub
        projects and found that a large fraction are abandoned shortly after
        creation - but abandoned projects are almost never studied, so we
        systematically over-estimate how healthy open-source communities are.
-   Hero developers [Majumder2019]: in many OSS projects, one person makes more
    than 80% of the commits. This concentration can itself be a confound: studies
    that compare "high-ownership" to "low-ownership" files may really be comparing
    "files the hero cares about" to "files nobody maintains."
-   He et al. [He2024] found that GitHub star counts - the most widely used
    popularity signal for repositories - are routinely faked by bots and
    purchased services, undermining studies that use stars as a proxy for quality
    or adoption.
-   Acciai et al. [Acciai2023] found systematic bias in who gets access to shared
    research data, meaning the datasets available to researchers are not a random
    sample of the datasets that exist.
-   Furia et al. [Furia2023] argue that SE studies typically report statistical
    correlations rather than causal relations, because running large-scale
    controlled experiments is often impossible. Treating a correlation as a
    causal claim is one of the most common validity threats in the field.
-   Observer effect: when programmers know they're being studied, they behave
    differently.
-   Self-reported data: surveys about hours worked, productivity, satisfaction
    are systematically biased.
-   External validity: does a study of university students generalize to industry?
    Does a study of open-source projects generalize to commercial software?

### Lesson 14: Non-Parametric Methods and Rank-Based Tests

Motivating question: Do educators correctly predict which programming mistakes

Replication target: Brown & Altadmri [Brown2017] - reproduce the finding that

types.

-   Brown & Altadmri [Brown2017] asked teachers to rank common novice mistakes
    and compared those rankings to actual data from the Blackbox system
    (millions of student submissions). Teachers were wrong in systematic ways.
-   Kruskal-Wallis test: non-parametric one-way ANOVA for comparing more than
    two groups.
-   Spearman rank correlation: comparing ranked lists without assuming that the
    distances between ranks are uniform.
-   Bootstrap resampling: the modern alternative to assuming a distribution.

    -   Works for almost any statistic, no distributional assumptions required.
-   When to bootstrap vs. when to use a parametric test.

### Lesson 15: Study Design and Replication

Motivating question: How do we know whether a study's conclusions are
trustworthy, and can we tell by trying to reproduce them?

Replication target: Participants compare their own Session 12 results (Fucci
TDD) to the numbers in the published paper. Any discrepancy becomes the entry
point for a discussion of what replication means, what "close enough" means, and
why published numbers sometimes can't be reproduced exactly.

-   Types of studies in SE research:
    -   Controlled experiments (lab and field).
    -   Case studies (one project, one company).
    -   Surveys and questionnaires.
    -   Mining studies (repositories, bug trackers, Stack Overflow).
    -   Systematic literature reviews.
-   What replication means and why it matters.
    -   Pizard et al. [Pizard2022] conducted a longitudinal study training
        students to read and replicate empirical SE studies. Students who went
        through the training were better at critiquing new claims and more likely
        to check statistical assumptions before accepting a result.
-   Pre-registration: committing to your hypotheses and methods before
    collecting data.
    -   Prevents p-hacking and hypothesis-after-results-known (HARKing).
-   The replication crisis in software engineering:
    -   Many "findings" in SE have not been replicated.
    -   Nagappan et al. [Nagappan2008a] found TDD reduced defects by 40-90%
        in industrial case studies; Fucci et al. [Fucci2016] found little effect
        in a controlled experiment. Both are valid. The settings differed.
-   Furia et al. [Furia2023] argue that observational data in SE supports only
    correlation claims. When a paper uses causal language ("X causes Y") but the
    study is observational, that is a validity threat the authors may not have
    acknowledged explicitly.
-   Reading a paper critically:
    -   What is the research question?
    -   What are the independent and dependent variables?
    -   What is the sample? How was it selected?
    -   What threats to validity do the authors acknowledge?
    -   What do they not acknowledge?

### Lesson 16: AI Tools and Software Engineering - What Do We Actually Know?

This session covers the emerging empirical literature on AI-assisted
development.  The field is moving fast; treat everything here as preliminary.

-   Productivity claims:
    -   Noda et al. [Noda2023] on DevEx: productivity is multidimensional
        (flow, feedback, cognitive load). Simple metrics (lines per day) miss most of it.
    -   GitHub's own studies report 55% faster task completion for isolated

you should put on a single published result.

Reflection 3: The workshop covered controlled experiments, observational studies,

Reflection 4: Noda et al. [Noda2023] argue that developer productivity is

## Day 4: Analyzing Qualitative Data

Day 4 covers qualitative and mixed-method research. Quantitative methods tell
you what happened and how often; qualitative methods tell you why. Both are
necessary for a complete picture of software engineering practice.

### Lesson 19: What Qualitative Data Looks Like in SE Research

Motivating question: Why do developers abandon projects they started?

-   Qualitative data in SE: interview transcripts, open survey responses, code
    review comments, issue tracker text, commit messages, Stack Overflow posts.
    -   Different from quantitative data: not counts or measurements, but language.
    -   The goal is to understand meaning, not frequency.
-   Hoda [Hoda2024] provides a practitioner-focused overview of qualitative
    methods in SE: types of data, collection techniques, and common pitfalls.
    -   Qualitative work is not "easier" than quantitative work. It is harder
        to do rigorously and harder to teach participants to trust.
-   When qualitative methods are the right choice:
    -   You do not yet know what the important variables are.
    -   The phenomenon involves judgment, motivation, or social context.
    -   Numbers exist but do not explain the pattern.
-   Ait et al. [Ait2022] show that most GitHub projects go inactive within a
    few years - but the survival data alone cannot explain why.
    -   Interviews with maintainers reveal: burnout, loss of interest, employer
        pressure, feeling underappreciated by users.
    -   The quantitative result raises the question; the qualitative result
        begins to answer it.
-   Concrete example: Silva et al. [Silva2016] analyzed developers' stated
    motivations for refactoring by reading 548 commit messages and classifying
    them into themes. The data source is quantitative (GitHub), but the
    analysis is qualitative (reading and interpreting text).

Exercise 1: Find five recent commit messages in any open-source repository on
GitHub. For each one, write one sentence describing the developer's apparent
motivation. Compare your interpretations with a partner. Where did you agree?
Where did you disagree, and why?

Exercise 2: The Ait et al. study counts inactive projects. Write three interview
questions you would ask a former maintainer to understand why they stopped
contributing. Make each question open-ended and non-leading.

Exercise 3: A colleague proposes to measure "developer satisfaction" by counting
positive and negative words in commit messages. Identify two ways this approach
might mislead you that a survey with open-ended questions would not.

Exercise 4: List three SE phenomena that you think quantitative methods handle
well and three that you think require qualitative investigation. For each
qualitative example, say what question you would be trying to answer.

Exercise 5: Read the abstract of Hoda [Hoda2024]. What does the author identify
as the most common mistake researchers make when analyzing qualitative data?
Write one sentence summarizing the mistake and one sentence explaining how you
would avoid it.

### Lesson 20: Thematic Analysis

Motivating question: What do developers actually find frustrating about code review?

-   Thematic analysis: a method for identifying, analyzing, and reporting
    patterns (themes) within textual data.
    -   Not the same as counting keywords. Themes capture meaning, not words.
    -   The researcher is the instrument. Transparency about choices matters.
-   The six-step process (Braun & Clarke):
    -   Familiarize yourself with the data: read everything, take notes.
    -   Generate initial codes: label segments with what they are about.
    -   Search for themes: group codes into candidate themes.
    -   Review themes: check that each theme holds together and is distinct.
    -   Define and name themes: write a clear description of each.
    -   Write up: use quotes from the data to illustrate each theme.
-   Aghajani et al. [Aghajani2019] analyzed 878 documentation artifacts from
    mailing lists, Stack Overflow, issue trackers, and pull requests. They
    identified themes such as "incorrect documentation" and "incomplete examples"
    through open reading, not keyword search.
-   Fan et al. [Fan2024b] used thematic analysis on community discussions about
    protestware (developers sabotaging their own packages). Themes included
    "normative disagreement" and "platform responsibility."
-   Deductive vs. inductive coding:
    -   Deductive: you start with a predefined framework and code against it.
    -   Inductive: themes emerge from the data without a prior framework.
    -   Most SE qualitative work is inductive on first pass, then deductive
        on replication.

Exercise 1: Read ten Stack Overflow comments on any Python or JavaScript
question. Generate at least five initial codes for what the comments are doing
(e.g., "corrects error in accepted answer," "asks for clarification"). Group
your codes into two or three candidate themes. Write one sentence describing
each theme.

Exercise 2: Aghajani et al. sorted documentation issues into categories. Pick
one category from their taxonomy and find three real examples of it in the
documentation of any open-source library you have used. Write one sentence per
example explaining why it fits the category.

Exercise 3: A study codes GitHub issue comments as "positive," "neutral," or
"negative" using a sentiment analysis library. Identify two types of comments
where automatic sentiment scoring would give the wrong label, and explain why.

Exercise 4: Thematic analysis requires the researcher to make interpretive
choices. Describe one choice you made in Exercise 1 where a different researcher
might have coded the same text differently. How would you document that choice
in a published paper?

Exercise 5: Find the threats-to-validity section of any qualitative SE paper.
What threat does the author identify most prominently? Is the proposed mitigation
convincing? Write three sentences evaluating it.

### Lesson 21: Coding Schemes and Inter-Rater Reliability

Motivating question: How do we know whether two researchers reading the same
data are seeing the same thing?

-   The coding problem: if only one person codes the data, the results reflect
    that person's interpretation, not necessarily the data.
    -   Solution: two independent coders, then measure agreement.
-   Cohen's kappa: agreement beyond chance.
    -   kappa = (observed agreement - expected agreement) / (1 - expected agreement)
    -   kappa < 0.4: poor; 0.4-0.6: moderate; 0.6-0.8: substantial; > 0.8: near-perfect.
    -   These thresholds are conventions, not laws. Report the number; let readers judge.
-   Krippendorff's alpha: handles more than two raters and ordinal/continuous codes.
-   Wang et al. [Wang2020a] used card sorting to classify reasons why bugs go
    unfixed in Mozilla, Eclipse, and Apache OpenOffice. Two researchers sorted
    independently; disagreements were resolved by discussion. They report kappa
    for each classification pass.
-   The codebook: a written description of each code with examples and
    non-examples. A good codebook lets a third party code the same data
    and reach the same conclusions.
-   Handling disagreements:
    -   Discuss until consensus (most common).
    -   Add a "borderline" category.
    -   Bring in a third coder as tiebreaker.
    -   Report unresolved disagreements as a limitation.
-   Common mistake: coding all the data together rather than independently,
    then claiming high agreement.

Exercise 1: Take the ten Stack Overflow comments you coded in Lesson 20. Give
the same comments to a partner who uses your codebook but has not discussed them
with you. Compute the percentage agreement and Cohen's kappa. Is the kappa above
0.6? If not, identify one code definition that was ambiguous and revise it.

Exercise 2: Write a three-code codebook for classifying pull request review
comments as "requesting a change," "asking a question," or "approving." Apply it
to ten real review comments from any public GitHub repository. Then give the
codebook and comments to a partner and compute kappa.

Exercise 3: Wang et al. report kappa values for each coding pass. Why might
kappa improve between the first and second pass? Give two reasons other than
"we practiced."

Exercise 4: A researcher codes 200 issue comments solo and reports 95% internal
consistency. What is wrong with this claim, and what should the researcher
have done instead?

Exercise 5: Compute the expected agreement for a two-category coding scheme
where coder A assigns 60% of items to category 1 and coder B assigns 70% to
category 1. Use this to compute the kappa you would need to achieve if the
observed agreement were 80%. Is that kappa acceptable by the common thresholds?

### Lesson 22: Grounded Theory (Practical Introduction)

Motivating question: What makes pair programming productive, and how would you
find out without knowing in advance what to look for?

-   Grounded theory (GT): a method for building theory from data rather than
    testing a pre-specified hypothesis.
    -   Appropriate when the phenomenon is not well understood and no existing
        theory fits.
    -   Produces a conceptual model, not a p-value.
-   The three coding stages (Strauss & Corbin version):
    -   Open coding: read the data and label concepts freely, without constraint.
    -   Axial coding: connect concepts by identifying causes, conditions, and
        consequences.
    -   Selective coding: identify the core category that integrates everything else.
-   Constant comparison: as you code new data, compare it to everything coded so far.
    -   This is what makes GT iterative, not just a fancy name for thematic analysis.
-   Theoretical saturation: stop collecting data when new interviews stop
    producing new concepts.
    -   Saturation is a judgment call, not a formula. Report the evidence for it.
-   Zieris & Prechelt [Zieris2021] used GT to study pair programming skill.
    They interviewed and observed professional developers and derived a model of
    what distinguishes productive from unproductive pairing, without starting
    from an existing taxonomy.
-   Barke et al. [Barke2019] used GT to study role conflict in self-organizing
    agile teams. Interviews revealed that role ambiguity was both a source of
    conflict and, paradoxically, a source of flexibility.
-   GT is frequently misused: claiming GT when you actually did thematic analysis
    is common in SE papers. The distinguishing mark is iterative data collection
    driven by emerging theory, not sequential collection followed by coding.

Exercise 1: Read the abstract of Zieris & Prechelt [Zieris2021]. What is the
core category they identify? Write one sentence explaining how that category
connects the other concepts in their model.

Exercise 2: You interview five developers about how they decide which bugs to fix
first. Your open codes include: "customer pressure," "ease of fix," "affects many
users," "personal interest," and "manager says so." Propose two axial codes that
group these into broader categories and explain your grouping.

Exercise 3: A paper claims to use grounded theory but collected all data before
any coding began. Explain in two sentences why this violates a core GT principle
and what consequence that has for the resulting theory.

Exercise 4: Theoretical saturation is often invoked without evidence. Design a
simple check: what would you record during data collection that would let a
reader judge whether saturation was actually reached?

Exercise 5: Compare thematic analysis (Lesson 20) and grounded theory (this
lesson) on three dimensions: starting point, output, and appropriate use case.
Write one sentence per dimension per method (six sentences total).

### Lesson 23: Interviews and Survey Open-Ends

Motivating question: How do practitioners actually decide whether to adopt a
new tool, and what would you need to ask to find out?

-   Semi-structured interviews: a prepared list of topics, but the interviewer
    follows the conversation rather than reading a script.
    -   Most common qualitative method in SE research.
    -   Preparation matters: pilot the questions with someone not in your sample.
-   Question types to use:
    -   Grand tour: "Walk me through the last time you reviewed someone's code."
    -   Experience: "Tell me about a time a tool surprised you."
    -   Probing: "Can you say more about that?" / "What happened next?"
-   Question types to avoid:
    -   Leading: "Don't you think pull requests improve quality?"
    -   Binary: "Do you use code reviews? Yes or no."
    -   Double-barreled: "How do you feel about pair programming and mob programming?"
-   Ameller et al. [Ameller2012] interviewed 18 developers at different companies
    about how they handle non-functional requirements in practice. The findings
    contradicted several textbook recommendations.
-   Survey open-ends: shorter responses, larger N, but less depth.
    -   Useful for validating or extending qualitative findings at scale.
    -   Begel & Zimmermann [Begel2014] combined structured ratings with open-ended
        responses to understand what practitioners wanted from SE researchers.
-   Sampling in qualitative work:
    -   Purposive sampling: select participants who have relevant experience.
    -   Snowball sampling: ask participants to refer others.
    -   Random sampling is rarely used and rarely appropriate.
-   Ethical considerations: consent, anonymization, right to withdraw, data
    retention. Gold & Krinke [Gold2020] extend these concerns to mined data
    where participants did not explicitly consent to being studied.

Exercise 1: Write five interview questions for a study about how developers
choose between writing tests before or after writing code. Ensure at least two
questions are grand-tour or experience questions, and none are leading.

Exercise 2: Take one of your questions from Exercise 1 and rewrite it as a
Likert-scale survey item (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). What
information does the quantitative version capture that the interview question
does not, and vice versa?

Exercise 3: Ameller et al. found that developers handle non-functional
requirements in ways that differ from textbook prescriptions. Pick one
non-functional requirement type (performance, security, usability) and write
two questions that would reveal how a developer actually handles it day-to-day,
without assuming they follow any particular process.

Exercise 4: A study recruits interview participants by posting on Reddit and
Twitter. Identify two ways this sampling strategy might bias the findings and
propose one mitigation for each.

Exercise 5: You conduct 12 interviews and your supervisor asks how you know you
have enough. Write a three-sentence response that explains theoretical saturation
in plain language and describes one piece of evidence from your interviews that
supports the claim.

### Lesson 24: Mixed Methods

Motivating question: A repository mining study finds that projects with more
contributors have fewer bugs per commit. How would you find out why?

-   Mixed methods: combining quantitative and qualitative data in a single study.
    -   The quantitative part answers "how much" and "how often."
    -   The qualitative part answers "why" and "in what context."
    -   Neither is subordinate; each compensates for the other's weaknesses.
-   Sequential designs:
    -   Quantitative then qualitative (QUAN -> qual): mine repositories to
        identify anomalies, then interview developers at those projects to
        explain them.
    -   Qualitative then quantitative (QUAL -> quan): interview developers to
        identify hypotheses, then test those hypotheses on a large dataset.
-   Concurrent (convergent) design: collect both types of data at the same time
    and compare results. Use when you want triangulation.
    -   Triangulation: if the numbers and the interviews point in the same
        direction, confidence increases. If they conflict, investigate why.
-   DeSouza Santos et al. [DeSouzaSantos2022] studied the impact of COVID-era
    remote work on software teams: a survey (quantitative) identified patterns,
    and interviews (qualitative) explained why those patterns emerged.
    -   Finding: many teams reported improved focus but worse onboarding for
        new hires. The interviews revealed the mechanism: async communication
        hides the informal knowledge-sharing that happens in offices.
-   Furia et al. [Furia2023] argue that observational SE data supports only
    predictive claims, not causal ones. Mixed methods can help bridge that gap:
    the qualitative component can reveal plausible causal mechanisms that the
    quantitative data cannot confirm alone.
-   Common pitfall: running the qualitative part as an afterthought to explain
    away inconvenient quantitative results. Design both components together.

Exercise 1: The repository mining study in the motivating question finds that
projects with more contributors have fewer bugs per commit. Propose a follow-up
interview study: who would you interview, what three questions would you ask,
and what finding would lead you to revise the quantitative result?

Exercise 2: DeSouza Santos et al. used a survey followed by interviews. Describe
one finding that the survey alone could not have produced and explain why the
interview component was necessary to obtain it.

Exercise 3: Design a concurrent mixed-methods study of a single question: "Do
developers read the documentation before filing a bug report?" Describe your
quantitative data source, your qualitative data source, and how you would handle
a case where they give contradictory answers.

Exercise 4: A paper combines log data (quantitative) with a survey (also
quantitative) and calls the result "mixed methods." Is this accurate? Explain in
two sentences what is missing and why it matters.

Exercise 5: Furia et al. argue that correlation data cannot establish causation.
Pick one finding from Day 2 of this workshop and describe a qualitative follow-up
study that would provide evidence (though not proof) for a causal interpretation
of that finding.
